# Case Interviews

Case interviews present an open-ended business problem and evaluate how you
**structure ambiguity**, perform mental math under pressure, and communicate
a defensible recommendation. Originating in management consulting, the format
has spread to product management, strategy, and operations roles where
structured problem-solving is the core job.

## The 4-Step Framework

Every case interview follows the same arc, regardless of the problem type:

| Step | What you do | Time budget | Common failure |
|------|-------------|-------------|----------------|
| **1. Clarify** | Restate the problem; ask about scope, objectives, constraints | 10% | Jumping to analysis without confirming the question |
| **2. Structure** | Break the problem into a MECE framework; lay out your approach | 15% | Unstructured brainstorming; no visible framework |
| **3. Analyze** | Work through the framework; do the math; test hypotheses | 60% | Getting lost in one branch; ignoring the clock |
| **4. Conclude** | Synthesize findings into a recommendation with risks | 15% | No conclusion; trailing off as time runs out |

### Step 1: Clarify the Question

The case opens with a prompt like "Our client is a coffee chain considering
entering the Chinese market." Your first job is not to solve — it is to
**narrow**. Ask:

- **What is the client's objective?** Revenue growth? Market share?
  Profitability?
- **What are the constraints?** Budget, timeline, regulatory?
- **What is the scope?** Geographic, product line, timeframe?

This step is not optional. Interviewers use it to evaluate whether you ask
the right questions before reaching for answers — a core consulting skill.

### Step 2: Structure the Approach

Before analyzing, lay out a **framework** — a visual decomposition of the
problem on paper or a whiteboard. The framework must be **MECE** (see below).
State your structure aloud:

> "I'd like to look at this in three buckets: market attractiveness, our
> client's capabilities, and the competitive landscape. Under market
> attractiveness, I'll look at market size, growth rate, and regulatory
> barriers. Under capabilities..."

The interviewer will either accept your structure or redirect you. Both
outcomes are fine — the point is that you demonstrated structured thinking.

### Step 3: Analyze

Work through your framework branch by branch. This is where the math happens
(see below). Key principles:

- **Follow the 80/20 rule.** Identify which branch of your framework is most
  likely to drive the answer and go there first.
- **Narrate your reasoning.** The interviewer needs to follow your logic in
  real time. Silence is a failure mode, same as in technical interviews.
- **Test hypotheses.** "I suspect the market is large enough to justify entry;
  let me size it to confirm."
- **Ask for data.** Interviewers have data they will share if you ask the
  right question. "Do we have any data on competitor market share?"

### Step 4: Conclude

Synthesize your analysis into a **recommendation** — not a summary of
calculations. The structure:

1. **The recommendation** — one clear sentence ("I recommend the client enter
   the Shanghai and Beijing markets with a premium positioning.")
2. **The top 2–3 reasons** — the evidence that supports the recommendation
3. **The key risks** — what could go wrong and how to mitigate
4. **Next steps** — what the client should do first

A case without a conclusion is a failed case, even if the analysis was
correct. Interviewers evaluate your ability to **decide**, not just analyze.

## The MECE Principle

**MECE** stands for **Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive**. It is the
gold standard for problem decomposition in consulting.

- **Mutually Exclusive** — no overlap between categories. If your framework
  has "revenue" and "online sales" as separate branches, it is not MECE
  because online sales are a subset of revenue.
- **Collectively Exhaustive** — no gaps. If your profitability framework
  covers revenue and fixed costs but omits variable costs, it is not
  exhaustive.

### MECE in Practice

| Problem | MECE framework | Non-MECE framework |
|---------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Profitability** | Revenue (price × volume) + Costs (fixed + variable) | "Revenue, costs, and marketing" (marketing overlaps with both) |
| **Market entry** | Market attractiveness + Competitive position + Entry feasibility | "Market, competitors, and whether we should" (vague, overlapping) |
| **Revenue decline** | Volume (customers × frequency × units) + Price (list price × discounts) | "Fewer customers and economic downturn" (causes, not structure) |

MECE is a **habit of mind**, not a formula. The same problem can be
decomposed multiple valid ways; the test is whether your decomposition is
clean and complete.

## Common Case Types

| Case type | What it tests | Example prompt |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Market sizing** | Estimation, assumptions, math | "How many diapers are sold in the US annually?" |
| **Profitability** | Root-cause analysis of profit decline | "Our client's profits are down 20% — why?" |
| **M&A / Due diligence** | Valuation, synergy assessment, risk | "Should our client acquire this competitor?" |
| **Market entry** | Market attractiveness, entry strategy | "Should a US retailer enter the Indian market?" |
| **Pricing strategy** | Price elasticity, willingness to pay | "How should we price our new SaaS product?" |
| **Operations / Process** | Bottleneck analysis, throughput | "Our factory's output has dropped 15% — fix it." |

### Market Sizing

Market sizing cases test your ability to **estimate from first principles**
when you have no data. The method:

1. **Top-down:** Start with a population or total market, then narrow with
   assumptions. "US population ~330M → households ~130M → households with
   babies ~4M → diapers per baby per day ~6 → annual diaper market ~8.6B
   units."
2. **Bottom-up:** Start with a single unit and scale up. "One baby uses 6
   diapers/day → 2,190/year → ~4M babies in the US → ~8.6B units."

Do both if time permits. If they converge, your estimate is robust. Round
numbers deliberately — "about 4 million" is better than "3.97 million"
because it signals comfort with approximation.

### Profitability Cases

The profitability framework is the most reusable structure in case
interviews:

```
Profit = Revenue − Costs
Revenue = Price × Volume
Costs = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs
```

When profits decline, decompose into these branches and ask "which moved?"
Then drill into the branch that moved. The interviewer has the answer hidden
in one branch; your job is to find it efficiently.

## Math Under Pressure

Case interview math is **mental math with round numbers**. You will not have
a calculator. The skills that matter:

| Skill | Example | Practice method |
|-------|---------|-----------------|
| **Multiplication with rounding** | 48 × 250 → 50 × 250 − 2 × 250 = 12,500 − 500 = 12,000 | Daily 10-minute drills |
| **Percentages** | 15% of 80M → 10% + 5% = 8M + 4M = 12M | Convert to fractions: 15% = 3/20 |
| **Growth rates** | 10% annual growth over 3 years → 1.1³ ≈ 1.33 | Memorize: 1.1²=1.21, 1.1³=1.33, 1.2²=1.44 |
| **Breakeven** | Fixed cost / contribution margin per unit | Practice the formula until automatic |

### Math Communication Rules

- **Announce your approach before calculating.** "I'm going to round 48 to
  50 and adjust at the end."
- **Talk through each step.** The interviewer needs to follow your arithmetic.
- **Sanity-check the result.** "12,000 units — does that make sense for a
  city of 500K? That's about 2.4% penetration, which seems reasonable."
- **Do not freeze on a wrong number.** If you catch an error, correct it
  aloud and move on. Interviewers respect recovery more than perfection.

## When Case Interviews Appear Outside Consulting

Case-style interviews increasingly appear in roles that require structured
business thinking:

| Role | Case style used | What it tests |
|------|----------------|---------------|
| **Product management** | Product design or strategy cases | User-centric thinking, prioritization, trade-offs |
| **Strategy / BizOps** | Standard consulting cases | Same as consulting |
| **Operations** | Process improvement cases | Bottleneck analysis, throughput optimization |
| **Data / Analytics leadership** | Business cases with data interpretation | Translating analysis into recommendations |
| **Corporate development** | M&A and valuation cases | Financial reasoning, synergy assessment |

For product management, the case often blends with a product design question:
"Design a product for [user] who needs [job]." The structure is the same —
clarify, structure (user segments, jobs-to-be-done, priorities), analyze,
conclude — but the content is product-centric rather than finance-centric.

## Preparation

| Resource | What it covers | Time investment |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Case in Point** (Cosentino) | Case frameworks, math drills, example cases | Read once; reference |
| **Case interview practice (peers)** | Live simulation under pressure | 2–3 sessions/week for 4 weeks |
| **Mental math apps** (e.g., Case Math) | Arithmetic speed drills | 10 min/day |
| **Victor Cheng's Case Interview Workshop** | Frameworks and delivery | Watch once; take notes |

The highest-leverage preparation is **live practice with a partner** who can
give feedback on your structure and communication. Solo practice builds math
fluency but not the social pressure of narrating to an observer.
