# Metrics and Quantification

## Why Metrics Matter

Resumes with specific numbers are **40% more likely** to result in an
interview. In a study of 125,484 resumes, 92% of recruiters surveyed stated
that metrics are a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Numbers serve three functions on a resume:

1. **Eye-catching anchors** — In a block of words, numbers break the visual
   pattern and draw the scanner's eye. This is especially important during the
   7.4-second recruiter scan.
2. **Proof of measurement capability** — Metrics demonstrate that you know
   how to measure and improve your own work, which signals operational
   maturity.
3. **Comparability** — Quantified achievements let a hiring manager compare
   your impact against other candidates. "Increased efficiency" is
   uncomparable; "Reduced API latency by 40% (from 500ms to 300ms)" is
   immediately comparable.

## The X-Y-Z Formula

Google's recommended bullet structure:

> **Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]**

- **X** = What you achieved (the outcome)
- **Y** = How you measured success (the metric)
- **Z** = How you did it (the method)

**Example:**
> Reduced cloud infrastructure spend by 34% ($1.2M annually) by migrating
> stateless services from EC2 to Spot instances with automated failover.

## How Many Metrics

Include **at least 5 measurable metrics** across the resume. Resumes with five
or more quantified results significantly outperform those with fewer.

## Quantifying Hard-to-Measure Work

Not every role produces clean numbers. When direct metrics are unavailable:

### Whole-Project Impact

If your specific output is hard to isolate (common for contractors or
internal-tool builders), list the impact of the entire project alongside your
contribution:

> Built the internal CI/CD migration tool that enabled the platform team to
> cut deployment time by 60% across 40 microservices.

### Technical-to-Product Translation

Don't just report technical metrics — connect them to product or business
impact:

| Too narrow | Better |
|------------|--------|
| Increased test coverage by 80% | Increased test coverage by 80%, reducing user-reported bugs by 50% |
| Migrated to new framework | Migrated to new framework, cutting page load time by 2.1s and improving conversion by 12% |

### Monetary Value

Whenever possible, mention how much money your work saved or earned:

> Automated the manual reporting pipeline, saving 20 engineering hours/week
> ($260K annualized labor cost redirected to feature work).

### Objective Signals When Numbers Are Unavailable

If you genuinely cannot produce numbers, use objective signals:
- Awards and recognitions
- New customers or accounts enabled
- Projects that unblocked other teams
- Speed of completion relative to expectations

## Common Mistakes

- **Vague percentages without context** — "Increased efficiency by 30%" is
  meaningless without a baseline. Always include the from/to or the absolute
  number.
- **Metrics without method** — Stating a result without explaining how you
  achieved it makes the claim unverifiable. The X-Y-Z formula's "Z" component
  is what makes the bullet credible.
- **Responsibility metrics vs. achievement metrics** — "Managed a $5M budget"
  is a responsibility. "Reduced budget overrun from 15% to 2% on a $5M annual
  budget" is an achievement.
