# Severance Negotiation

## The Core Insight

When you leave under duress — a layoff, a termination, or a negotiated mutual
separation — the employer typically presents a **separation agreement** and
asks you to sign it. Most people sign it immediately, in shock. This is a
mistake. The agreement is a **negotiation**, not a take-it-or-leave-it
ultimatum, and the terms you accept will shape your financial runway and your
reputation for years.

> The decision to separate is usually final. The terms of the separation
> almost never are.

## What's Negotiable

| Term | Often negotiable? | Typical leverage |
|------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Severance pay (weeks per year of service) | Yes | Tenure, seniority, role criticality, reason for separation |
| PTO payout | Sometimes | State law may mandate it; otherwise negotiable |
| COBRA continuation (duration / subsidy) | Yes | Especially for the first 3–6 months of job search |
| Outplacement services | Yes | Career coaching, resume help, office space |
| Vesting acceleration | Yes, at senior levels | Equity cliff, single-trigger vs. double-trigger |
| Non-disparagement clause | Yes — make it mutual | You protect your reputation; they protect theirs |
| Reference agreement | Yes | A agreed-upon reference script or confirmation-of-employment only |
| Garden leave | Yes, at senior levels | Continued pay during a non-work notice period |
| Extended benefits coverage | Sometimes | Health, life, disability beyond COBRA |
| Job-search support / introductions | Sometimes | Especially in a "no-fault" layoff |

### Severance Pay — The Baseline

There is no legal minimum severance in most U.S. jurisdictions (unless
contractually guaranteed). The informal standard is **1–2 weeks of pay per
year of service**, with senior roles often commanding more. Treat any initial
offer as an **anchor**, not a ceiling.

| Role level | Typical initial offer | Reasonable target |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Individual contributor | 1 week / year | 2 weeks / year + benefits bridge |
| Senior IC / lead | 2 weeks / year | 3–4 weeks / year + outplacement |
| Manager / director | 2–4 weeks / year | 4–8 weeks / year + vesting review |
| Executive | Per contract | Negotiate via counsel; often 6–12 months |

## What's Typically Non-Negotiable

| Term | Why it's fixed |
|------|---------------|
| The decision to separate | The employer has made the call; you cannot un-fire yourself |
| The effective termination date | Operational and legal finality |
| Return of company property | Mandatory; non-compliance invites legal action |
| Non-compete (if already in your contract) | Pre-existing obligation; renegotiate only with leverage |
| Final wages earned | Dictated by state law, not negotiation |

Spend your negotiating energy on the negotiable terms. Fighting the decision
itself — unless you have a discrimination or wrongful-termination claim —
wastes the leverage you do have.

## The Separation Agreement — Don't Sign Immediately

The agreement will ask you to release claims against the employer in exchange
for the severance. This is a **legal waiver of your rights**. Do not sign it on
the spot, no matter how much pressure you feel.

### Statutory Review Periods (U.S.)

| Law / situation | Review period | Revocation period |
|----------------|---------------|-------------------|
| OWBPA (workers 40+) | 21 days to consider (45 days for group layoffs) | 7 days to revoke after signing |
| Individual under 40 | No federal minimum; often "reasonable" — push for at least 5–7 days | Varies by state |
| State-specific protections | Varies | Check your state |

Under the **Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA)**, workers 40 and over
must be given **21 days** to review a separation agreement (45 days for a
group layoff) and **7 days to revoke** after signing. If the employer pressures
you to sign faster, that pressure itself is a red flag.

### What to Do During the Review Period

1. **Read the entire agreement.** Every clause, especially the release of
   claims, non-disparagement, non-solicit, and confidentiality provisions.
2. **Inventory what you're giving up.** Claims for discrimination, unpaid
   wages, unpaid equity, wrongful termination.
3. **Identify what's missing.** Is there a reference agreement? Outplacement?
   Vesting acceleration?
4. **Prepare a counter.** Itemize the terms you want improved.
5. **Consult a lawyer if any red flag appears** (see below).

## When to Involve an Employment Lawyer

| Situation | Why you need counsel |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Red flags in the agreement | Overbroad non-compete, hidden non-solicit, waiver of unpaid wages |
| Potential discrimination claim | Age, gender, race, disability, retaliation — these have real value |
| Significant equity at stake | Unvested RSUs, options, founder equity — the math is complex |
| Executive separation | The dollar amounts justify the legal cost many times over |
| Pressure to sign immediately | A lawyer levels the power dynamic |
| Non-compete enforceability question | State law varies widely; you need a local opinion |

A one- or two-hour consultation with an employment plaintiff's lawyer typically
costs a few hundred dollars and can identify six-figure issues you would have
missed. For executives, the lawyer often pays for themselves out of the
improved package.

## The "Garden Leave" Concept

**Garden leave** is a negotiated period during which you remain technically
employed (on payroll, with benefits) but are not required to work — and
usually cannot start a new job until it ends. It is common at senior levels
and in finance, sales, and certain tech roles.

| Feature | Garden leave | Standard severance |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Employment status | Still employed | Terminated |
| Pay | Regular salary continues | Lump sum or scheduled payments |
| Benefits | Continue as normal | COBRA / separate |
| Can you start a new job? | No — not until leave ends | Yes — immediately |
| Non-compete clock | Runs during leave | May or may not run |

Garden leave is valuable because it extends your benefits coverage and gives
you a paid runway to job-search. If offered, scrutinize the restrictions; if
not offered, it is sometimes negotiable at senior levels.

## Negotiating Tactics

- **Don't accept the first offer verbally.** Thank them, take the agreement,
  say you will review it. Silence is leverage.
- **Anchor on total value, not individual line items.** "The total package
  needs to bridge a 6-month job search" is stronger than "I want 2 more
  weeks of severance."
- **Trade, don't demand.** "I can sign quickly if we can agree on X and Y"
  gives the employer a reason to move.
- **Get the reference agreement in writing.** A verbal "we'll say good
  things" is worthless. Specify the script or the confirmation-only policy.
- **Make non-disparagement mutual.** You agree not to disparage them; they
  agree not to disparage you. This protects you from rogue managers.

## What NOT to Do

- **Don't sign under pressure.** The review period exists for a reason.
- **Don't threaten litigation you don't intend to pursue.** It shuts down
  negotiation and invites the legal team to harden the offer.
- **Don't take company data as "insurance."** This converts a negotiation
  into a criminal matter instantly.
- **Don't vent about the separation on social media.** A non-disparagement
  clause you haven't signed yet can still be invoked, and future employers
  read your feed.
- **Don't ignore the tax implications.** Severance is usually taxed as
  supplemental wages; a CPA can help structure it (e.g., into the next tax
  year if you are near a bracket boundary).
