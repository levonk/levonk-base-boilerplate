# Recruiter Communication

## Recruiters Are Professional Matchmakers

Recruiters take a candidate's resume, look at it, and based on the intake
meeting with the hiring manager (where they learn what the hiring manager
needs), they do **pattern matching** to determine if the person looks like
the right fit on paper. If they are, that is when they talk to the
candidate to make sure they are actually a match.

### The Intake Meeting

Before a role is posted, the recruiter has an intake meeting with the
hiring manager. In this meeting, they learn:
- What the team does
- What gaps exist
- What skills and experience the ideal candidate has
- What the must-have vs. nice-to-have qualifications are

The recruiter then pattern-matches incoming resumes against this intake
profile. This is why **mirroring the job description's language matters**
(see [Keyword Engineering](keyword-engineering.md)) — the recruiter is
literally scanning for the words from the intake meeting.

## Recruiters Are Professional Stalkers

If you want a recruiter to "professionally stalk" you, optimize your
profile for discovery. Recruiters actively search LinkedIn and other
platforms for candidates who match their intake profiles — even before a
role is posted publicly.

### Optimizing for Recruiter Discovery

| Platform | What recruiters search | How to optimize |
|----------|----------------------|-----------------|
| LinkedIn | Job titles, skills, keywords | Use target-role titles; embed keywords in headline and experience |
| GitHub | Tech stack, project quality | Pin repos that demonstrate relevant skills; clean READMEs |
| Personal site | Thought leadership, depth | Publish case studies relevant to your target role |
| Conference talks | Domain expertise, visibility | Speak at events in your target domain |

### The LinkedIn Discovery Loop

1. Recruiter searches LinkedIn for "VP Engineering" + "AI/ML" + "Los Angeles"
2. Your profile appears in results because your headline and experience
   match
3. Recruiter sends a connection request or InMail
4. You respond within 24 hours (recruiters move fast — see OE/OA in
   [Interview Strategy](interview-strategy.md))
5. Initial screening call
6. If match, fast-track through the interview process

### What Makes You Discoverable

- **Headline matches target roles** — Not your current title if it doesn't
  translate; use a functional title (see [Title Gap Bridging](title-gap-bridging.md))
- **Keywords in the right places** — Headline, summary, skills section, and
  experience descriptions (see [Keyword Engineering](keyword-engineering.md))
- **Complete profile** — A bare profile decreases callbacks (see
  [LinkedIn Profile Optimization](linkedin-profile-optimization.md))
- **Active engagement** — Posting and commenting keeps you in the feed
  algorithm
- **Connection density** — Connections with people at target companies
  increase your visibility in recruiter searches

## Working With Recruiters Throughout the Process

### Initial Screening Call

The screening call is not a formality — it is the first pattern-matching
test. The recruiter is checking:
- Does this person sound like what they claimed on their resume?
- Do they communicate clearly?
- Are they genuinely interested in this role, or just fishing?
- Are their salary expectations in range?

**Prepare for the screening call as seriously as for a technical interview.**
Know the role, the company, and your pitch.

### Asking the Right Questions

During the screening call and throughout the process, ask:

- "What is the hiring manager looking for in this role?"
- "What gaps exist on the team?"
- "What does the interview process look like? How many rounds?"
- "What is the timeline for this role?"
- "Is this a backfill or a new role?"

These questions give you information to position yourself in later rounds
and signal that you are engaged and thoughtful.

### Staying Top of Mind

Recruiters juggle 10–20 roles at a time. To stay top of mind:

- **Respond within 24 hours** to every communication
- **Send a thank-you note** after every interview round
- **Provide requested materials promptly** (references, work samples)
- **Don't go silent** — If you need time to decide, say so with a date

### When to Walk Away

Red flags that signal you should decline to continue:

- The recruiter is adversarial or pushy about salary
- The process is disorganized with no clear timeline
- The hiring manager is disrespectful in the interview
- The role description keeps shifting
- The company has a pattern of bad reviews on Glassdoor/Blind

Trust your instincts. A bad recruiting process often reflects a bad
internal culture.

## Information Boundaries: What Not to Share with Agency Recruiters

Agency recruiters often work "full desk" — they handle both candidate
recruitment and business development (finding new clients). They are not
your therapists or career coaches; they are wearing a "sales hat" with a
vacancy to fill and a client to satisfy. Every detail you provide helps
them map the market, identify which companies are hiring, and determine
market rates.

### The Interview Lead Trap

Sharing where you are interviewing is essentially handing over a
high-value sales lead. If you tell an agency recruiter you are in the
second round with "Manager Tom" at "Company X," the recruiter may call
that company to pitch a "stronger" candidate — creating competition for
yourself. Agencies train consultants to extract this information because
knowing which companies are actively hiring is how they build their
business.

### The Confessional Booth Trap

Candidates often unload frustrations about micromanagement, failed
product launches, or conflicts at their current jobs. Oversharing hands
over power — every detail helps the recruiter assess your desperation
level and negotiate against you.

### Three Deflection Scripts

When a recruiter presses for your interview activity:

1. **Vague but casual:**
   > "Yeah, I've got a few things progressing. I'm keeping my options open
   > while focusing on the right fit."

2. **Boundary with empathy:**
   > "You'll probably hate this, but I've been asked to keep their identity
   > private as it's a confidential search. But don't worry — I'd never
   > want to share my interview activity set up by yourself if the tables
   > were turned."

3. **Reverse psychology:**
   > "Totally understand if knowing the company is a must. I totally
   > respect you. Would you rather hold off on this one?"

### What to Never Share

- Specific interview activity — company names, hiring manager names,
  interview stages ("I'm in the third round")
- A complete breakdown of your current job's failures
- Frustrations with other recruiters (telling a recruiter "recruiters
  never read resumes" is not a strong play)

### What You CAN Share

- **Salary expectations** — Unlike interview activity, this is useful for
  agency recruiters to know and does not harm your search in the same way
  (see [Salary Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md) for how to frame this)
- **General industry direction** — "I'm focusing on fintech and healthtech
  roles" is fine; naming specific companies is not

### Communication Best Practices

- **Protect your intel.** Treat your interview list as a valuable business
  asset.
- **Be polite, professional, and warm** while remaining unshakable on
  privacy.
- **Project confidence and control.** Recruiters are more attracted to
  candidates who seem to have other options and firm boundaries.
- **Maintain a strategic partnership mindset.** Stop treating the recruiter
  as a career coach or therapist.
