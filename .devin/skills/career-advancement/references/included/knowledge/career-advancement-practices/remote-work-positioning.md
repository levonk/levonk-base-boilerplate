# Remote Work Positioning

## Framing Yourself for Remote and Distributed Roles

Remote work is not a perk — it is a **distinct working mode** that requires
demonstrably different skills. Companies hiring for remote roles screen for
evidence that you can operate without proximity, supervision, and synchronous
collaboration. **Framing yourself for remote roles means surfacing the signals
that prove you can deliver in a distributed environment.**

## Remote-Ready Signals on a Resume

Recruiters and hiring managers scanning for remote-capable candidates look for
**specific evidence** in your resume. These signals must be explicit — do not
assume that any in-office experience translates.

| Signal | What It Proves | How to Surface It |
|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Explicit remote experience** | You have actually worked remotely, not just want to | "Led a fully remote team of 12 across 4 time zones" |
| **Async communication tools** | You operate in written, asynchronous workflows | Slack, Loom, Notion, Linear, GitHub PRs in experience entries |
| **Distributed team leadership** | You can lead people you cannot see daily | "Managed engineers in SF, Berlin, and Bangalore" |
| **Self-directed delivery** | You can ship without daily oversight | "Owned end-to-end delivery of X with quarterly check-ins" |
| **Documentation culture** | You write things down instead of relying on meetings | "Authored team RFC process; reduced sync meetings by 40%" |

**If you have remote experience and it is not on your resume, you are
underpositioning.** Make it explicit in the experience entry, not just in a
skills list.

## The Remote Interview

Remote interviews test **two things simultaneously**: your qualifications and
your ability to operate in the remote medium itself. The interview is itself a
demonstration of remote readiness.

### Video Etiquette

- **Camera on** — This is the default expectation in remote-first companies.
  Camera-off signals disengagement or unreadiness for remote collaboration.
- **Look at the camera** when speaking, not at the screen — this simulates eye
  contact
- **Lighting and framing** — Face well-lit; face centered in frame; neutral
  background
- **No multitasking** — No email, no Slack, no phone visible. Remote
  interviewers can tell when you are distracted.

### Handling Technical Issues Gracefully

Technical issues are **inevitable** in remote interviews. How you handle them
is a live test of your remote composure.

| Issue | Wrong Response | Right Response |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Audio drops | Panic, freeze, go silent | "I lost audio for a moment — can you repeat the last question?" |
| Video freezes | Apologize profusely, lose your train of thought | Switch to phone audio if needed; stay calm; resume seamlessly |
| Screen share fails | Fumble for 5 minutes | "Let me try another approach" — have a backup (PDF, shared link) |
| Lag in conversation | Talk over each other repeatedly | Pause an extra beat; acknowledge the lag; adjust rhythm |

**Graceful handling of technical issues is a stronger signal than no issues at
all** — it demonstrates the exact composure remote work demands.

### The Home Office Setup

- **Dedicated, quiet space** — Not a coffee shop, not a couch with TV in
  background
- **Reliable internet** — Wired connection or strong Wi-Fi; have a mobile
  hotspot backup
- **Quality audio** — A dedicated microphone or headset beats laptop built-in
  audio
- **Backup plan** — Phone with data for audio fallback; know how to join via
  mobile if needed

## Demonstrating Async Communication Skills

Remote-first companies operate on **asynchronous communication** — written,
durable, time-shifted. The interview process is an opportunity to demonstrate
this skill live.

- **Thoughtful written follow-ups** — After every interview round, send a
  concise, well-structured thank-you email that references specific discussion
  points. This demonstrates written communication ability.
- **Clear documentation** — If asked to prepare a case study or take-home,
  include a written README or executive summary. The quality of your written
  artifacts is itself a signal.
- **Proactive updates** — If a deliverable will be late, communicate early with
  a revised timeline. Do not go silent and hope it goes unnoticed.
- **Structured written responses** — When sending materials, use clear
  headings, bullet points, and scannable structure. Dense walls of text signal
  that you write for yourself, not for a remote reader.

| Async Signal | Demonstrated In Interview By |
|--------------|------------------------------|
| Clear written communication | Well-structured follow-up emails, documented take-homes |
| Proactive status updates | Early communication about delays or clarifying questions |
| Documentation habit | READMEs, written case studies, structured responses |
| Self-direction | Completing take-homes without hand-holding; asking precise questions |

## Remote-First vs Remote-Friendly

This distinction is **critical** and often misunderstood. It determines
whether the company's culture will actually support remote work or merely
tolerate it.

| Dimension | Remote-First | Remote-Friendly |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Culture origin** | Built for remote from day one | Office culture with remote as accommodation |
| **Communication** | Async-default; written documentation primary | Sync-default; meetings primary; notes optional |
| **Decision-making** | Documented in writing; accessible to all time zones | Made in office; remote workers find out later |
| **Career advancement** | Remote and in-office employees advance equally | Remote employees face proximity bias; slower advancement |
| **Meeting norms** | Few meetings; async updates; recorded sessions | Frequent sync meetings; remote workers must attend on office hours |
| **Documentation** | Comprehensive; the source of truth | Sparse; institutional knowledge lives in office conversations |

**Remote-first companies are where remote workers thrive. Remote-friendly
companies are where remote workers survive.** If long-term remote career
growth matters, prioritize remote-first employers.

## Red Flags in Remote Job Postings

Not every "remote" job is genuinely remote. Watch for these signals:

| Red Flag | What It Likely Means |
|----------|---------------------|
| **No remote policy stated** | Company hasn't thought about remote work; you'll be an experiment |
| **"Must be in [city] occasionally" without specifics** | Undefined in-office expectations; will expand over time |
| **Surveillance software mentioned** (activity tracking, keystroke logging, screenshot monitoring) | Trust deficit; toxic remote culture; you will be micromanaged |
| **"Remote until [date]"** | Temporary accommodation; role will return to office |
| **Core hours spanning only one time zone** | Not truly distributed; you must align to HQ time zone |
| **No remote employees in leadership** | Proximity bias is structural; remote workers will hit a ceiling |

## The Overcommunication Principle

The single most important behavioral shift for remote workers is
**overcommunication** — providing more context, more updates, and more
visibility than feels necessary in an office environment.

In an office, presence is implicit communication. Remotely, **if you don't
write it down, it didn't happen.**

- **Status updates** — Proactively share progress, blockers, and next steps
  without being asked
- **Context in writing** — Decisions, rationale, and trade-offs documented
  where the team can find them
- **Visibility of work** — Use public channels, not DMs, so others can see
  what is happening
- **Explicit availability** — Share your working hours, time zone, and
  response-time expectations

**Overcommunication is not noise — it is the remote equivalent of office
presence.** Undercommunicating remotely is the same as being absent in an
office.
