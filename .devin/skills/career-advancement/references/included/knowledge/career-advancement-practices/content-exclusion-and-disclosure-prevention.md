# Content Exclusion and Disclosure Prevention

## The Core Principle

A resume, cover letter, LinkedIn profile, or any professional document is a
**filtered representation** of your professional self. It is not a biography,
a confession, or a personal statement. Every item included must serve the
hiring signal. Every item excluded protects you from bias, liability, and
reputational damage.

**When in doubt, leave it out.** No hiring decision has ever been reversed
because a candidate included too little personal information. Many have been
reversed because a candidate included too much.

## Protected-Class Information (Never Include)

In the United States, federal law (EEOC) prohibits employment discrimination
based on protected characteristics. Including protected-class information in
a resume **gives the employer ammunition to discriminate and then claim a
different reason** for the rejection. Even if the employer is unbiased, the
presence of this information creates legal exposure for them — which makes
them less likely to hire you.

### Never Include

| Category | What to exclude | Why |
|----------|----------------|-----|
| **Race / ethnicity** | Photos that reveal race, racial identifiers, ethnic organizations (unless professionally relevant) | Protected under Title VII; creates bias and employer liability |
| **Religion** | Religious affiliation, religious activities, faith-based volunteering (unless applying to a religious organization) | Protected under Title VII; can trigger cultural-fit bias |
| **Gender / sexual orientation** | Gender pronouns in resume (LinkedIn is different — see below), sexual orientation disclosures | Protected or semi-protected; can trigger bias in conservative markets |
| **Age** | Graduation years, early-career dates, "20+ years experience" phrasing | Protected over 40 (ADEA); see [Age Bias Coded Language](age-bias-coded-language.md) |
| **National origin** | Birthplace, citizenship status (unless the job requires it), visa status details | Protected under Title VII; can trigger bias |
| **Disability** | Medical conditions, accommodations needed, disability disclosures (unless requesting accommodation through proper channels) | Protected under ADA; disclose through HR channels, not the resume |
| **Marital / family status** | "Married with two children," spouse's name, family status | Protected in many states; creates assumptions about availability and focus |
| **Pregnancy** | Pregnancy status, maternity leave | Protected under PWFA; triggers assumptions about availability |

### LinkedIn Nuance

LinkedIn is a **public profile**, not a resume. Some protected-class
information is normative on LinkedIn but not on a resume:

- **Photos** are expected on LinkedIn (and recommended — see [LinkedIn Profile
  Optimization](linkedin-profile-optimization.md)) but **forbidden on US
  resumes** (see [ATS Reality vs Myths](ats-reality-vs-myths.md))
- **Pronouns** are increasingly normative on LinkedIn but should be omitted
  from resumes in most US markets
- **Volunteer work** including faith-based or identity-based organizations is
  acceptable on LinkedIn but should be curated on a resume

When in doubt, match the platform norm, not your personal preference.

## Controversial Topics (Never Include)

### Politics

- **Never** include political party affiliation, campaign work, political
  donations, or partisan statements
- **Never** reference political figures, slogans, or hashtags
- **Exception**: Non-partisan civic engagement (e.g., "Voter Registration
  Volunteer, City of X") is acceptable if it demonstrates relevant skills
  (organizing, logistics) without partisan framing
- **Why**: Political affiliation creates immediate in-group/out-group bias. A
  hiring manager with opposite views will screen you out before reading your
  qualifications. This is legal in most US states (political affiliation is
  not a federally protected class), so you have no recourse.

### Religion (Detailed)

- **Never** include religious denomination, church membership, or faith-based
  activities unless applying to a religious organization where it is directly
  relevant
- **Never** use religious language or quotes in a resume or cover letter
- **Exception**: Skills-based volunteering at a religious organization (e.g.,
  "Built a database for a community food bank") is acceptable if framed by
  the skill, not the faith
- **Why**: Religious disclosure triggers cultural-fit bias and creates
  assumptions about values, availability (holidays), and worldview

### Firearms and Weapons

- **Never** reference firearms ownership, shooting sports, hunting, or
  weapons-related hobbies
- **Never** reference military weapons training unless applying to a
  defense/security role where it is directly relevant
- **Why**: Firearms are a polarizing topic in the US. References trigger
  strong emotional reactions that override professional evaluation

### Alcohol and Tobacco

- **Never** reference alcohol consumption, drinking culture, bar work
  (unless applying to hospitality), or drinking-related hobbies
- **Never** reference tobacco, cigarettes, vaping, or nicotine use
- **Never** reference partying, nightlife, or social drinking
- **Exception**: Wine/spirits expertise is acceptable **only** when applying
  to food/beverage/hospitality roles where it is a professional qualification
- **Why**: Substance references signal lifestyle choices that may conflict
  with company culture, health insurance costs, or reliability assumptions

### Cannabis and Other Substances

- **Never** reference cannabis use, even in legal jurisdictions
- **Never** reference any drug use, past or present
- **Never** reference recovery, rehab, or sobriety (protected under ADA in
  some contexts, but disclosure on a resume creates bias)
- **Why**: Despite legalization, substance references create reliability and
  judgment questions. Disclose through HR channels only if accommodation is
  needed

## Personal and Private Information

### Never Include

| Item | Why |
|------|-----|
| **Social Security Number** | Identity theft risk; no legitimate employer needs it before an offer |
| **Full home address** | Privacy risk; city/state is sufficient for geographic signal |
| **Driver's license number** | Identity theft risk; only needed for background checks post-offer |
| **Passport number** | Identity theft risk; only needed for I-9 verification post-offer |
| **Bank account or financial information** | Fraud risk; never needed pre-offer |
| **Health insurance information** | Privacy risk; only needed post-offer |
| **Personal references' contact info** | Privacy risk for your references; provide only when requested |
| **Salary history** | Banned in many states; provides no benefit to you (see [Salary Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md)) |

### Minimal Contact Information

A resume needs exactly:

- **Name**
- **Phone number** (one)
- **Email** (one, professional — see below)
- **Location** (city/state or "Remote")
- **LinkedIn URL** (optional but recommended)

Anything beyond this is unnecessary and creates privacy exposure.

## Unprofessional Content

### Email Addresses

- **Never** use personal email addresses that reveal hobbies, nicknames,
  birth years, or personal details (e.g., `skaterdude2004@...`,
  `partygirl1980@...`)
- **Use** a professional format: `firstname.lastname@domain.com` or
  `firstinitial.lastname@domain.com`
- **Never** use an email that reveals political, religious, or controversial
  affiliations

### Social Media Links

- **Never** link to personal social media (Instagram, TikTok, Facebook,
  personal X/Twitter) unless it is a professional portfolio
- **Never** link to a public profile that contains controversial content,
  even if the link itself is professional
- **Audit** your public social media before applying — recruiters will search
  your name (see [LinkedIn Profile Optimization](linkedin-profile-optimization.md)
  for the LinkedIn audit checklist)

### Hobbies and Interests

- **Generally omit** from resumes — they waste space and create bias
- **Never** include hobbies that reveal protected-class information or
  controversial topics:
  - Religious activities
  - Political activism
  - Hunting/shooting
  - Drinking/wine tasting (unless hospitality-relevant)
  - Religious or political book clubs
- **Exception**: A hobby that directly demonstrates a relevant skill (e.g.,
  open-source contributing for a software role) can be included, framed as
  professional development, not as a hobby

## Negative Content

### Never Include

- **Negative commentary about previous employers** — signals disloyalty and
  poor judgment
- **Reasons for leaving** a job on the resume — save for the interview, framed
  positively
- **Gaps explained defensively** — use "Independent Consulting" or "Strategic
  Sabbatical" without elaboration (see [Conciseness and Length](conciseness-and-length.md))
- **Salary complaints or disputes** — signals conflict and entitlement
- **Performance issues or criticisms** — never self-disclose weaknesses
- **Legal disputes or lawsuits** — creates immediate risk perception

### Framing Negative History

If you have a gap, a short tenure, or a layoff:

- **State it factually** without emotional language
- **Focus on what you did** during the gap (consulting, learning, projects)
- **Never explain why** on the resume — save for the interview
- **Never blame** a former employer, manager, or colleague

## The Disclosure Audit

Before submitting any professional document, run this audit:

### Resume / Cover Letter Audit

- [ ] No photos or physical descriptions
- [ ] No graduation years (unless under 30 and it is an advantage)
- [ ] No race, religion, gender, age, disability, marital status, or
      national-origin references
- [ ] No political affiliations or partisan language
- [ ] No alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, or substance references
- [ ] No firearms or weapons references
- [ ] No negative commentary about previous employers
- [ ] No personal social media links
- [ ] No SSN, full address, or financial information
- [ ] Professional email address only
- [ ] No hobbies that reveal controversial topics or protected-class info

### LinkedIn Audit

- [ ] Public posts reviewed for controversial content
- [ ] No political rants or partisan content visible
- [ ] No alcohol/partying photos visible
- [ ] No controversial religious content (faith affiliation in profile is
      acceptable; proselytizing posts are not)
- [ ] Activity history reviewed — comments and shares are visible
- [ ] Connections list does not expose you to guilt-by-association bias

### The "Forward to a Stranger" Test

Show your resume to someone who does not know you. Ask: "What can you infer
about my personal life, beliefs, or background from this document?" If they
can infer any protected-class information, controversial topic, or personal
detail that is not professionally relevant, **remove it**.

## Why This Matters

Including excluded content does not just risk bias — it signals **poor
judgment**. A hiring manager who sees a political reference on a resume
thinks: "If this person cannot filter their professional documents, how will
they filter their communication with clients, executives, and the public?"

The ability to present a clean, focused, professional document is itself a
signal of executive maturity. Excluding irrelevant and risky content is not
hiding — it is **curating for the audience and the purpose**.
