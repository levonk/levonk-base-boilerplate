# Reference Management

## Who to List as References

References are people who can speak to your work, character, and
capabilities from direct experience. The strongest references are those
with the most relevant perspective on the role you are applying for.

### The Reference Hierarchy

| Reference type | Value | When to include |
|----------------|-------|-----------------|
| **Direct manager** | Highest — they can speak to performance, growth, and day-to-day work | Always include at least one if possible |
| **Peer / colleague** | High — speaks to collaboration and technical ability | Include for team-fit validation |
| **Direct report** | High — for leadership roles, demonstrates management capability | Essential for management and leadership positions |
| **Cross-functional partner** | Medium — speaks to influence and collaboration outside your team | Useful for roles requiring stakeholder management |
| **Client / customer** | Medium — speaks to external-facing impact | Useful for sales, consulting, customer-facing roles |
| **Personal friend** | None — not a professional reference | Never include |

### For Leadership Roles

If you are applying for a management or leadership role, include at least
one **direct report** as a reference. Companies evaluating leadership
candidates want to hear from people who reported to you. A reference set
that includes only managers and peers — but no reports — is a signal that
you may not want your management style scrutinized by those who experienced
it.

### How Many References

| Number | Use case |
|--------|----------|
| 3 | Standard for most roles |
| 5 | Senior or executive roles, or when the employer requests more |
| Fewer than 3 | Not enough — the employer cannot cross-reference |

## How to Prepare References

A reference who is caught off guard is a weak reference. Preparing your
references is not optional — it is basic professional courtesy and directly
affects the quality of the reference.

### Preparation Checklist

| Step | What to do |
|------|-----------|
| **Ask permission** | Never list someone without asking. An surprised reference is a damaged relationship. |
| **Tell them about the role** | Share the job description, company name, and why you are interested. |
| **Share your resume** | Remind them of what you did while you worked together so their memory is fresh. |
| **Brief them on key talking points** | Highlight 2–3 achievements or skills you want them to emphasize — without scripting them. |
| **Tell them who will call** | Share the name and role of the person who will contact them, if you know it. |
| **Give them a heads-up when it happens** | Let them know the moment you hear a reference check is underway so they are not surprised by the call. |

### The Preparation Message

> "Hi [name] — I'm interviewing for a [role] at [company] and would love to
> list you as a reference. The role focuses on [key responsibility], and I
> think you'd be able to speak to [specific project / skill we worked on
> together]. I've attached the job description and my current resume for
> context. Would you be comfortable serving as a reference? No pressure at
> all if the timing isn't right."

## What References Will Be Asked

Understanding what the employer will ask your references helps you prepare
them effectively. Typical reference questions include:

| Question area | What they are really assessing |
|---------------|-------------------------------|
| **Strengths** | Whether your self-assessment matches external perception |
| **Weaknesses / areas for growth** | Whether you have blind spots and whether you are coachable |
| **Would you rehire?** | The single most important question — a "no" or a hedge is a red flag |
| **Work style** | How you operate day-to-day — autonomy, collaboration, communication |
| **Specific achievements** | Whether the accomplishments on your resume are real and accurately described |
| **Reason for departure** | Whether the story you told matches the reference's understanding |
| **Interpersonal dynamics** | Whether you are easy to work with or a source of friction |

The **"would you rehire?"** question is the one that matters most. A
reference who hesitates or says "it depends" is effectively a negative
reference. This is why you must ask your references — privately and
honestly — whether they would rehire you before you list them.

## The "References Available Upon Request" Debate

| Practice | Recommendation |
|----------|----------------|
| Including "References available upon request" on the resume | **Do not.** It is outdated, takes up space, and states the obvious — of course references are available. |
| Including reference names and contact info on the resume | **Do not.** Protect your references' privacy. Share them only when the employer asks, typically after an offer is extended or contingent on references. |
| Having a prepared reference list document | **Yes.** Keep a separate document with 3–5 references, their relationship to you, current title, company, email, and phone. Send it when asked. |

The modern standard: do not include references on the resume, but have
them ready to send within minutes of being asked.

## How to Coach References Without Scripting Them

There is a line between **preparing** a reference and **scripting** a
reference. Preparation is professional. Scripting is dishonest, and a good
reference checker can tell the difference — scripted answers sound
rehearsed and lack the natural specificity of genuine recollection.

### What to Do

- Share the job description so they understand what the employer needs
- Highlight 2–3 achievements you want them to be ready to discuss
- Remind them of the context of your work together (dates, projects, your
  role)
- Tell them what you are hoping the employer takes away

### What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it backfires |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Giving them a script** | A scripted reference sounds hollow and raises suspicion |
| **Telling them what to say about weaknesses** | If the weakness answer sounds rehearsed, the checker assumes you are hiding something worse |
| **Asking them to lie or exaggerate** | This is fraud, and a reference who agrees to it is not someone you want in your corner |
| **Over-preparing to the point of anxiety** | A nervous reference is almost as bad as a bad one. Keep preparation light and conversational. |

### The Coaching Call

A 10-minute call is usually enough. Walk through the role, remind them of
your work together, and ask: "Is there anything you think I should be
ready for them to ask?" This question does two things — it prepares them,
and it surfaces any concerns they have that you might want to address
before the check happens.

## After the Reference Check

| Action | Why |
|--------|-----|
| **Thank your references** | They did you a favor. A thank you (email or note) is mandatory. |
| **Update them on the outcome** | Whether you got the offer or not, close the loop. References hate being left in the dark. |
| **Reciprocate** | If appropriate, offer to serve as a reference for them in the future. |
