# Age Bias Coded Language

## How Age Bias Manifests

Age discrimination is rarely direct. It is coded in subtle language — both in
job descriptions and in recruiter feedback. Recognizing the coded language
helps you navigate it strategically rather than being blindsided.

## Coded Phrases and Their Meanings

| Coded phrase | What it actually means |
|-------------|----------------------|
| "Fast-paced environment" | "Can the older candidate keep up?" |
| "High energy" | "We want younger staff" |
| "Cultural fit" | "We want someone who matches the existing (younger) team's demographics" |
| "We need someone hungry" | "We want someone early in their career who will work long hours" |
| "Digital native" | "We want someone who grew up with technology" |
| "Recent graduate energy" | Direct age filtering (illegal in many jurisdictions) |
| "We're looking for a builder" | "We don't want someone who manages — we want someone who codes" |

## The Managerial Fears Behind the Bias

Age bias is not always malicious. It often stems from specific managerial
fears:

### 1. Intimidation

Younger managers may fear that an experienced hire will challenge their
authority or make them look inexperienced by comparison.

### 2. Budgetary Concerns

Assumptions that an older candidate will automatically demand a higher salary
than budgeted, even when the candidate has not stated salary expectations.

### 3. Longevity

Fears that an older candidate will retire sooner, leave for a "final" role,
or not stay long enough to justify onboarding investment.

### 4. Adaptability

Assumptions that older candidates are "set in their ways" and won't adopt new
tools, processes, or methodologies.

### 5. Reporting Structure

Discomfort with the idea of a younger manager supervising someone older enough
to be their parent.

## Resume Strategy: Curation Over Hiding

Hiding your age entirely is not the solution — it is often detectable and can
create the impression of dishonesty. Instead, **curate your narrative for
relevance**:

- **Trim irrelevant experience** — You do not need to list every job you've
  ever had. Focus on the last 10–15 years.
- **Tell a relevant story** — Ensure every included role directly supports
  your candidacy for the specific job.
- **Remove age-signaling details** — Graduation years, first roles from the
  1990s, legacy technologies (see [Proving Ground
  Principle](proving-ground-principle.md) for the trimming framework).

## Proving Adaptability on the Resume

The "adaptability" fear is the most addressable on a resume. Counter it by:

- **Mentioning recent certifications** — Specifically name modern tools
  earned within the last year
- **Referencing current industry trends** — AI-driven insights, modern
  frameworks, current methodologies
- **Showing continuous exploration** — Current projects or tools you are
  actively exploring
- **Using current terminology** — Avoid legacy tech names that signal you
  haven't kept current

## Interview Strategy: Proactive Surfacing

Don't wait for the recruiter to wonder about fit — give them a chance to voice
concerns so you can address them:

- **Ask the elaboration question:** "Is there any area where I could
  elaborate on how I would approach this role?"
- **Address adaptability directly:** Cite a specific recent example (e.g.,
  "implemented an agile process that increased efficiency by 25% last
  quarter")
- **Position as solutions-focused:** Show that your experience allows you to
  hit the ground running without handholding
