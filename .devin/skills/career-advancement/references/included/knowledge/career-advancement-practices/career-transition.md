# Career Transition

## The Core Insight

A career transition is not a restart — it is a **reframe**. The mistake most
career changers make is treating the pivot as if they are starting from zero,
which is exactly how the market will read them. The successful pivoter treats
the transition as a continuation: a story that has been building toward this
moment, with a skill set that bridges the old domain and the new one.

> You are not changing careers. Your career has been building toward this.

The market does not reward courage or reinvention. It rewards **relevance**.
Your job during a transition is to make the new domain's hiring managers see
you as a safe pair of hands — not a risky bet on a hopeful newcomer (see
[Safe Pair of Hands Positioning](safe-pair-of-hands-positioning.md)).

## The Bridge Strategy

Do not try to leap the full distance between two domains in one jump. Find the
**overlapping skill set** — the capabilities that live in both the old and new
domains — and build your positioning around that intersection.

| Old domain | New domain | The bridge (overlap) |
|------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Data engineering | ML platform | Distributed systems, data pipelines, infra reliability |
| Finance analyst | Product manager | Quantitative modeling, stakeholder tradeoff analysis |
| Support engineering | Developer relations | Technical communication, debugging, tooling |
| Journalist | Content marketing | Narrative craft, audience research, editorial process |
| Military logistics | Supply chain ops | Large-scale coordination, contingency planning, vendor mgmt |

The bridge is what makes you credible. Everything outside the bridge is a
**domain knowledge gap** you must close separately (see below).

## Building Credibility in the New Domain

The new domain will not grant you credibility because you want it. You have to
**earn visible evidence** before you apply. This is the same evidence-over-
experience principle applied to a full industry change (see [Evidence Over
Experience](evidence-over-experience.md)).

| Credibility builder | Why it works | How long it takes |
|---------------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Certification | Signals current, codified knowledge | 1–3 months |
| Side project / portfolio | Proof of work in the new domain | 1–6 months |
| Volunteer / pro-bono work | Real outcomes in the new domain, references | 2–6 months |
| Writing / talks | Demonstrates thought leadership and depth | Ongoing |
| Open-source contributions | Public, verifiable proof of capability | Ongoing |
| Contract / freelance work | Paid evidence in the new domain | 3–12 months |

Pick **two or three**, not all of them. A certification plus a side project
plus one published write-up is a far stronger signal than six half-finished
initiatives.

## The Narrative Pivot

The story you tell about your own career is the single most important asset in
a transition. The wrong frame — "I'm changing careers" — invites the hiring
manager to treat you as a beginner. The right frame — "my career has been
building toward this" — invites them to treat you as an experienced hire with
a fresh perspective.

### The Wrong Frame

> "After 10 years in finance, I've decided to pivot into product management."

This reads as: *I woke up one day and decided to start over.* It positions you
as a junior candidate with no track record.

### The Right Frame

> "My career has been building toward product management. Ten years in finance
> taught me how to model tradeoffs and read markets; my last three years
> focused on cross-functional roadmap work because that's where the highest-
> leverage decisions live. I'm now ready to bring that quantitative foundation
> to a dedicated product role."

This reads as: *I have been preparing for this for years.* It positions you as
an experienced hire whose past is an asset, not a detour.

### The Pivot Formula

1. **Name the through-line** — the skill or theme that connects old to new.
2. **Show the trajectory** — point to recent work that already leans toward the
   new domain.
3. **State the destination** — declare the target role as the natural next
   step, not a departure.

## Transferable Skills Translation Framework

This is the framework from [Safe Pair of Hands Positioning](safe-pair-of-hands-positioning.md),
applied to a full industry change rather than a single role change. The stakes
are higher because the domain gap is wider.

### The Translation Process

1. **Remove old-domain jargon.** Every industry has its own dialect. Terms
   that sound impressive inside your old domain are meaningless outside it.
2. **Translate to the new domain's business priorities.** Frame every
   accomplishment in the language the new hiring manager uses to measure
   success.
3. **Map to their problems, not your achievements.** The question is never
   "what did I do?" — it is "which of their problems can I already solve?"

| Old-domain language (bad) | Translated for new domain (good) |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------|
| "Managed a $2B trading book" | "Owned risk-weighted allocation decisions across a $2B portfolio, directly analogous to product portfolio prioritization under resource constraints" |
| "Led quarterly close process" | "Ran a cross-functional, time-boxed delivery cycle with 5 teams and hard external deadlines" |
| "Built credit risk models" | "Built predictive models that translated ambiguous signals into go/no-go decisions — the same judgment product managers apply to feature bets" |

## The Stepping-Stone Approach

When the gap between old and new is too wide for a single jump, use an
**intermediate role** that bridges both domains. This is the most reliable
transition strategy because each step is a small, credible move rather than a
large, implausible one.

| Step | Role | Why it works |
|------|------|-------------|
| 1 | Role in old domain, but adjacent to new | You already have credibility; start doing new-domain work |
| 2 | Hybrid role spanning both domains | You are now a verified bridge candidate |
| 3 | Pure role in new domain | The hybrid role is your proof; the pivot is complete |

Example: a data engineer pivoting to ML platform engineering might first take
a data-platform role that includes ML-serving infrastructure, then move to a
dedicated ML platform team.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | What happens | The fix |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Applying with old-industry framing | Resume reads as foreign; instant rejection | Full translation pass before any application |
| Underestimating the domain knowledge gap | You pass the skills screen but fail the domain interview | Invest in credibility builders before applying |
| Overestimating transferable skills | You assume "leadership is leadership" and skip the bridge work | Map the overlap honestly; close the gap explicitly |
| Leaping instead of stepping | One giant jump with no intermediate proof | Use the stepping-stone approach |
| Apologizing for the pivot | You signal uncertainty; the manager reads risk | Use the narrative pivot; own the story |
| Networking only in the old domain | You stay invisible to the new domain's hiring managers | Redirect 80% of networking to the new domain |

## When the Transition Is Not Working

If after 6–9 months of credible effort you are not getting traction, the most
likely cause is one of:

1. **The bridge is too narrow** — the overlap between old and new is genuinely
   thin. Consider a closer adjacent domain first.
2. **The domain gap is too deep** — the new domain requires tacit knowledge
   that only comes from doing the work. Take a step down in seniority to get
   inside the domain, then climb back.
3. **The narrative is still off** — you are still framing the move as a
   departure instead of a continuation. Rewrite the story.
