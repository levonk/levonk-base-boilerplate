# Standard Interview Questions

Most interviews open with a small set of predictable questions. Candidates
fail them not because the questions are hard, but because they answer
**unprepared** — rambling, oversharing, or signaling the wrong thing. These
questions are not traps; they are opportunities to land the [Safe Pair of
Hands](safe-pair-of-hands-positioning.md) signal in the first five minutes
(see [Interview Strategy — The Five-Minute
Decision](interview-strategy.md#the-five-minute-decision-confirmation-bias)).

## The Principle: Frameworks, Not Scripts

Memorize the **structure** of each answer, not the words. A memorized script
sounds rehearsed and breaks under follow-up questions. A framework lets you
adapt to the specific company and role while staying grounded in the
underlying principle.

Every framework below has two layers:

- **Best answer** — when you have a specific, true hook for this company or
  role. Use it. Specificity wins.
- **Generic fallback** — when you don't have a specific hook. Use the
  general framework. A clean general answer beats a rambling specific one
  that isn't quite right.

The fallback is not a lesser answer — it is the **safe default** that keeps
you credible when you cannot personalize. The goal of the fallback is to be
forward-looking, role-relevant, and free of red flags.

## 1. "How are you?" — The Opening

This is not small talk. It is the first data point in the five-minute
decision. A flat, monosyllabic, or defensive answer starts the interviewer
digging for reasons to reject. A warm, brief, forward-looking answer starts
them digging for reasons to want you.

### Best answer

Warm, genuine, one sentence, then pivot to the conversation:

> "Doing well, thanks — really looking forward to this conversation. I've
> been [reading about / thinking about] [specific thing about the role or
> company], and I'm glad to dig into it."

### Generic fallback

> "Doing well, thanks. Really glad to be here — I've been looking forward to
> this conversation."

### What not to say

- "Not bad." / "Okay." / "Hanging in there." — Flat, signals low energy.
- A complaint or health update — Signals you will bring friction.
- A long monologue about your week — Signals you don't read the room.

## 2. "Why are you leaving your current role?"

This is the most common trap question. The interviewer is listening for
negativity, conflict, or desperation — all of which signal risk (see
[Recruiter Communication — The Confessional Booth
Trap](recruiter-communication.md#the-confessional-booth-trap)).

### The rule: pull factor, not push factor

Frame the move around what you are moving **toward**, not what you are
escaping. A pull factor ("I want [specific thing this role offers]") signals
growth and intention. A push factor ("my current job is bad because…")
signals conflict and grievance.

### Best answer

Name a specific attribute of the target role that your current role lacks,
then connect it to your trajectory:

> "I've had a great run at [current company] — I built [specific thing] and
> shipped [specific outcome]. What I'm looking for next is [specific
> challenge this role offers, e.g. 'owning platform reliability at scale'],
> and this role is one of the few places doing that seriously. That's what
> drew me to it."

### Generic fallback

> "I've learned a lot in my current role and I'm proud of what I've shipped
> there. I'm ready for the next challenge — specifically [growth direction
> the target role represents, e.g. 'moving from team lead to owning a larger
> platform'] — and this role is a strong fit for that direction."

### What not to say

- "My manager is terrible." / "I'm underpaid." / "The company is failing."
  — Any negativity reads as conflict risk.
- "There's no growth there." — Even if true, frame it as "I've grown
  everything I can there" rather than "they won't let me grow."
- "I was laid off." said defensively — If you were laid off, say it plainly
  and move on (see the gap question below).

## 3. "What have you been doing the last few years / over the break?" (Employment Gap)

A gap is not a disqualifier. Defensive, evasive, or apologetic answers are.
The interviewer wants to know: were you productive, or were you idle and
disengaged?

### Best answer

Name concrete, productive activity — paid work, learning, projects,
caregiving, or recovery — and connect it to readiness now:

> "After [previous role] ended in [month], I took [duration] to [specific
> productive activity: completed [cert/course], built [project], supported
> a family member through a health issue]. I'm now fully ready to return to
> [the kind of work this role is], and the time away actually sharpened
> [specific relevant skill or perspective]."

### Generic fallback

> "I used the time deliberately — [learning / building / consulting /
> addressing a family situation] — and I'm now ready to bring that back to
> a full-time role like this one. The break gave me [a specific skill or
> perspective relevant to the role], which I'm eager to apply."

### What not to say

- "Looking for work." as the whole answer — Implies idle waiting.
- Apologizing or minimizing — "It's been a tough market…" reads as
  defeated.
- Overexplaining the circumstances of the departure — One clean sentence,
  then pivot to what you did with the time.

## 4. "Why our company?"

A generic answer here is a red flag — it signals you applied to 50
companies and remember none of them. Specificity is the entire point (see
[Application Strategy](application-strategy.md)).

### Best answer

Reference something specific you researched — a product, a recent
decision, an engineering blog post, a value, a customer — and connect it to
your work:

> "Three things drew me specifically. First, [specific product or
> technical decision] — I read [specific source] and it resonated because
> [connection to your work]. Second, [specific value or principle the
> company publishes] — that's how I've operated at [past role]. Third, the
> [specific challenge this role owns] is exactly the problem I want to work
> on next."

### Generic fallback

When you could not research deeply, use a three-part framework: mission
alignment + specific challenge + role fit:

> "I'm drawn to [company]'s mission around [stated mission]. The specific
> challenge this role owns — [restate the core problem from the job
> description] — is one I've worked adjacent to and want to own directly. And
> the role fits the direction I want to grow, which is [growth direction]."

### What not to say

- "You're a great company." / "I've always wanted to work here." —
  Generic, applies to anyone, signals no research.
- "The benefits." / "The location." — Transactional, signals you'll leave
  for a better deal.
- A long recitation of their website — Signals you read it on the way in.

## 5. "Where do you want to be in 5 years?"

The interviewer is checking for two failure modes: too vague ("happy here,
learning") signals no ambition; too specific and ambitious ("your job")
signals flight risk or threat. The safe answer shows a growth direction
that the company's own career ladder supports.

### Best answer

Name a direction the company's own career path enables, anchored in the
work this role does:

> "In five years I want to be [deeper in this craft / broader in scope] —
> specifically [a direction the company's ladder supports, e.g. 'owning the
> platform end-to-end' or 'leading a larger team doing this kind of work'].
> This role is the right next step toward that because [specific skill or
> scope it builds]."

### Generic fallback

> "I want to be [deeper expert / broader leader] in [the domain this role is
> in], working on problems with more [scope / impact / complexity] than I do
> today. I'm looking for a place where that growth happens in-house rather
> than by job-hopping, which is why I'm being deliberate about this choice."

### What not to say

- "I'm not sure." / "I just want to be happy." — Signals no direction.
- "In your seat." / "Running this place." — Signals threat or naivety.
- "Starting my own company." — Signals you'll leave.

## 6. "Why are you applying to a position you're overqualified for?"

This question tests for flight risk and for whether the step is deliberate
or desperate. The [Value Multiplier Reframe](value-multiplier-reframe.md)
applies directly: reposition the step as a deliberate choice, not a
settlement.

### Best answer

Name a specific reason this role — not any role — is the right fit, and
signal stability:

> "I'm deliberately choosing a role focused on [specific thing this role
> does] because [specific reason: 'I want to go deep on this craft rather
> than manage broadly,' or 'I want to ship this specific kind of work
> again']. My broader background is an asset here, not a stepping stone — I
> can [specific contribution the extra experience enables, e.g. 'mentor the
> team' or 'see around corners on the platform']. I'm looking for a place
> to do this work well for a long time, not a layover."

### Generic fallback

> "I'm making a deliberate choice to focus on [the core work this role
> does] rather than continue up a management track. The extra experience I
> bring means I can be productive faster and help the people around me —
> that's a better fit for me than a role where I'd be managing instead of
> doing. I'm looking for a long-term home, not a stopgap."

### What not to say

- "I just need a job." — Signals desperation and flight risk.
- "I'm tired of managing." — Signals grievance.
- Anything that implies you'll leave the moment a senior role opens —
  Flight risk is the exact fear this question is probing.

## 7. "Why are you applying to a position you're not qualified for?"

This is a stretch application. The interviewer is testing whether you
understand the gap and have a credible plan to close it. The [Safe Pair of
Hands](safe-pair-of-hands-positioning.md) transferable-skills framework
applies: translate adjacent experience into evidence you can ramp fast.

### Best answer

Acknowledge the gap honestly, then map adjacent experience onto it and name
a ramp plan:

> "You're right that I haven't done [specific missing qualification] in
> production. Here's why I'm a credible candidate anyway: at [past role] I
> did [adjacent experience that transfers] — the underlying skill is the
> same, the surface is different. My ramp plan is [specific: 'I've already
> started [course/project]' or 'I'd pair with [role] for the first 30
> days']. I've ramped into new domains before — [specific past example] —
> and I expect to do it again here."

### Generic fallback

> "I know I don't have [specific qualification] on paper. What I do have is
> [adjacent skill] and a track record of ramping into new areas quickly —
> at [past role] I came in not knowing [thing] and was productive within
> [timeframe]. I learn fast, I ask good questions early, and I'm prepared to
> put in the work to close the gap. I'd rather apply for a role I can grow
> into than one I've already outgrown."

### What not to say

- "I'm a fast learner." with no evidence — Unsubstantiated, signals
  hand-waving.
- Pretending the gap doesn't exist — Signals you didn't read the job
  description or you're dishonest.
- "I can do anything." — Signals no self-awareness.

## 8. "How do you like to manage people?"

Have a real philosophy. "I'm flexible" or "I treat everyone the same"
signals you've never thought about it — which signals you've never managed,
or that you manage badly. A concrete philosophy signals you've reflected on
what works.

### Best answer

Name your philosophy, give the one-sentence rationale, then a concrete
example:

> "I manage as a servant leader — my job is to unblock the team, set clear
> outcomes, and get out of the way on the how. Concretely: I set
> [quarterly outcomes] with each person, meet weekly to remove blockers,
> and review against outcomes rather than activity. At [past role] that
> let [specific person] grow into [specific result] without me
> micromanaging it."

### Generic fallback

> "I manage for outcomes, not activity. I set clear expectations up front,
> give people autonomy on the how, and check in regularly to remove
> blockers and course-correct. I'd rather a person own a problem and
> stretch than have me hand them a task list. When someone is struggling I
> move closer; when they're crushing it I move further away."

### What not to say

- "I'm a hands-off manager." — Often means "I disappear."
- "I'm flexible — it depends on the person." — True but empty; says
  nothing about your defaults.
- "I lead by example." — A cliché that signals no real philosophy.

## 9. "How do you like to be managed?"

This tests self-awareness and whether you'll thrive under this manager's
actual style. The honest answer that also fits the company's culture wins.
If you genuinely need a style the company doesn't offer, that's useful
information for both sides.

### Best answer

Name your needs concretely and tie them to outcomes:

> "I work best with clear outcomes, regular feedback, and autonomy on
> execution. Specifically: I want to know what success looks like for the
> quarter, get feedback early and often rather than only at review time,
> and own the how. I don't need hand-holding, but I do need a manager who
> tells me when I'm off-track — I'd rather hear it in week 2 than week 12."

### Generic fallback

> "I like clear expectations, regular feedback, and room to own the
> execution. Tell me what good looks like, tell me when I'm missing it, and
> let me figure out the how. I'd rather be corrected early and often than
> praised at the end."

### What not to say

- "I'm easy to manage." — Empty.
- "I need a lot of autonomy." with no specifics — Can read as "I don't
  want to be managed at all."
- A style that contradicts the company's actual culture — If you need
  heavy structure and they're a flat startup, say so honestly rather than
  pretending.

## 10. "How do you communicate?"

Abstract answers ("I'm a great communicator") fail. Concrete answers about
medium, cadence, and escalation win. The interviewer is checking whether
you'll be a [Safe Pair of Hands](safe-pair-of-hands-positioning.md) in
information flow, not a black box or a firehose.

### Best answer

Name your defaults for medium, cadence, and escalation, with a concrete
example:

> "I match the medium to the message. Status and quick decisions go in the
> team channel; anything nuanced or sensitive goes in writing with context;
> anything urgent or blocking goes in a call with a written follow-up. I
> over-communicate early on a new project and taper as trust builds. At
> [past role] that meant [specific example: 'a weekly written status that
> flagged risks early, which let us avoid a specific incident']. When
> something is blocked I escalate the same day, not the same week."

### Generic fallback

> "I match the medium to the message — chat for quick things, writing for
> anything with nuance, a call for anything urgent or blocking. I
> over-communicate early on new work and pull back as trust builds. And I
> escalate blockers the day I find them, not the day before they blow up."

### What not to say

- "I'm a great communicator." — Abstract, unsubstantiated.
- "I keep people informed." — Says nothing about how.
- "I prefer email." as a blanket answer — Signals rigidity.

## 11. "What questions do you have for me?" — The Audience-Aware Question

This is the one question you are **guaranteed** to be asked, in some form, by
every person you speak to. It is also the most underprepared. "No, I think
you covered everything" is a hard fail — it signals disengagement, lack of
curiosity, or fear of asking a dumb question. The right questions vary by
**who is asking**, because each person in the loop is evaluating you against
a different set of concerns.

### The meta-strategy

1. **Always have questions.** Two to three prepared, plus one that emerged
   from the conversation itself. Never zero.
2. **Match the question to the audience.** Asking the recruiter a deep
   technical architecture question signals you don't understand their role.
   Asking the skip-manager about day-to-day tooling signals you can't see
   the strategic layer.
3. **Ask at least one question that emerged from this conversation.** It
   shows active listening. "You mentioned [X] earlier — can you tell me
   more about how that works?" beats any pre-prepared question.
4. **Don't ask what the website already answers.** "How many employees
   does the company have?" signals zero research.
5. **Close with a forward-looking question.** End on next steps or
   trajectory, not on a backward-looking detail.

### Recruiter screening call

The recruiter is evaluating: pattern match, communication, salary fit,
process fit. They do not know the technical details of the role.

**Best questions:**
- "What is the hiring manager looking for in this role? What gaps exist
  on the team?"
- "What does the interview process look like? How many rounds, and what's
  the timeline?"
- "Is this a backfill or a new role? What level is it scoped at?"
- "What does an average offer look like for this level?" (see [Salary
  Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md))

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd love to understand what the hiring manager is most looking for in
> this role, and what the interview process looks like from here."

**What not to ask:** Deep technical questions, team architecture details,
specific engineering challenges — the recruiter won't know and it signals
you don't understand their role in the process.

### Subordinate (potential direct report interviewing you)

This person is evaluating: **Will I want to work for you?** They are
checking for psychological safety, micromanagement risk, mentorship
capacity, and whether you'll make their life better or worse.

**Best questions:**
- "What's the team doing well right now that I should make sure not to
  disrupt?"
- "What's frustrating about how things work today that you'd want a new
  [manager / lead / director] to fix?"
- "How does the team handle onboarding for new members? What would you
  want to see done differently?"
- "What's something you're proud of shipping recently?"
- "How do you like to be managed — close direction, or autonomy with
  checkpoints?"

**Generic fallback:**
> "What's the team doing well that I should preserve, and what's
> frustrating that you'd want addressed? And how do you like to be
> managed — I want to make sure I'd be a good fit for how you work best."

**What not to ask:** Questions that sound like a performance review ("What
are your weaknesses?"), questions about their salary, or questions that
signal you'll be a micromanager ("How do you track your hours?").

### Peer (fellow IC or same-level manager)

This person is evaluating: **Will I want to collaborate with you?** They
are checking for competence, communication, ego, and whether you'll pull
your weight or create friction.

**Best questions:**
- "What does the team do well? Where could it use reinforcement?"
- "How does the team make decisions? How is work prioritized?"
- "What's the most rewarding part of working here?"
- "What's the hardest part of working across teams here — and how does
  this role fit into making that better?"
- "How does information flow between this team and [adjacent team]?"

**Generic fallback:**
> "What does the team do well that I should learn from, and where could
> it use reinforcement? And how does the team make decisions and
> prioritize work — I want to understand how I'd plug in."

**What not to ask:** Questions about their career dissatisfaction (puts
them on the spot), questions about office politics, or questions that
sound like you're sizing them up as competition.

### Manager (hiring manager — your prospective boss)

This person is evaluating: **Can you do the job, and will you make my life
easier?** They are the primary decision-maker. They want to see that you
understand the problem they're hiring you to solve.

**Best questions:**
- "What gaps does the team have? How might my background fit those gaps
  based on what you've learned about me?"
- "What does success look like in the first 90 days? First year?"
- "What is the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "What's a decision you made recently that you'd make differently if you
  could redo it?" (signals you care about their judgment, not just the
  role)
- "How do you measure success for this role — what would tell you, six
  months in, that this was the right hire?"

**Generic fallback:**
> "What does success look like in the first 90 days, and what's the
> biggest challenge the team is facing that this hire needs to address?
> I want to make sure I'm solving the right problem."

**What not to ask:** Questions about compensation (that's the recruiter's
domain), questions about the manager's management style that sound like a
test ("Are you a micromanager?"), or questions so generic they could apply
to any company.

### Skip-manager (hiring manager's manager — VP/SVP level)

This person is evaluating: **Do you think at the right altitude?** They
care about strategy, business impact, and organizational fit — not
day-to-day execution. Asking about tooling or daily standups signals you
can't see the strategic layer.

**Best questions:**
- "What's the biggest strategic bet this org is making in the next 12–18
  months, and how does this role contribute to it?"
- "What does success look like for this team at the [VP / SVP] level —
  not just for this role, but for the org it sits in?"
- "Where does this team's work have the most impact on the business? Is
  that where you want it to be, or is there a gap?"
- "What's the thing you wish more people on this team understood about
  how the business actually works?"
- "How does this team's roadmap get funded and prioritized against other
  teams — what's the competition for resources?"

**Generic fallback:**
> "What's the biggest strategic priority for this org over the next year,
> and how does this role fit into it? I want to make sure I'm aligned
> with where the business is actually going, not just the team's
> day-to-day."

**What not to ask:** Tactical questions about tooling, process, or daily
workflow (that's the manager's or peers' domain), questions about the
skip-manager's personal career path, or questions that signal you can't
operate at the strategic level.

### Cross-audience: the question that works everywhere

If you can only prepare one question that is safe with any audience:

> "What does success look like for this role — and what would tell you,
> six months in, that this was the right hire?"

This works because: the recruiter can answer at the process level, the
subordinate can answer at the collaboration level, the peer can answer at
the team-contribution level, the manager can answer at the outcomes level,
and the skip-manager can answer at the strategic level. It adapts to the
person answering.

### The closing question (any audience)

End every interview with a forward-looking question:

> "What are the next steps from here, and is there anything else you'd
> like me to share or clarify before we wrap up?"

This signals engagement, gives you the timeline, and opens the door to
address any lingering concern before the conversation ends.

## 12. "What concerns do you have about this role?" — The Audience-Aware Concern

This is the mirror image of §11. When an interviewer asks if you have
concerns, the right concern depends on **who can address it**. Raising a
strategic concern to a subordinate wastes it — they can't act on it. Raising
a tactical concern to a skip-manager signals you can't see the strategic
layer. The mismatch is the failure mode, not the concern itself.

### The meta-strategy

1. **Always have one concern.** "No concerns" signals naivety or
   disengagement — a thoughtful candidate always has something they want to
   understand better.
2. **Frame it as a question, not a complaint.** "I'm curious about…" or "I'd
   want to understand…" beats "I'm worried about…" — the former signals
   curiosity, the latter signals negativity.
3. **Match the concern to the audience.** Raise what that person can
   actually address or speak to.
4. **Have a follow-up ready.** If they answer your concern, acknowledge it
   and either let it go or ask one more — don't belabor it.
5. **Don't raise dealbreakers here.** If something is a genuine dealbreaker
   (compensation far below your floor, a toxic signal you observed), raise
   it with the recruiter privately, not in a group interview.

### Recruiter screening call

The recruiter can speak to: process, timeline, compensation band, role
level, basic role mechanics. They cannot speak to team dynamics, technical
architecture, or strategic direction.

**Best concerns:**
- "I'd want to understand the timeline for this role — is there urgency to
  fill it, or is it a planned expansion?"
- "What level is this scoped at, and is there flexibility on level if the
  fit is strong?"
- "What does the compensation band look like for this level?" (see [Salary
  Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md))
- "Is this a backfill or a new role? That affects how I'd think about the
  first 90 days."

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd want to understand the timeline and whether this is a backfill or a
> new role — that shapes how I'd think about ramp-up."

**What not to raise:** Team culture concerns, technical architecture
questions, strategic direction — the recruiter won't know, and it signals
you don't understand their role.

### Subordinate (potential direct report)

The subordinate can speak to: day-to-day reality, team morale, how the
manager actually operates, what's frustrating. They cannot speak to
strategy, budget, or org-level decisions.

**Best concerns:**
- "I'd want to understand how the team feels about the current direction —
  is there buy-in, or is there friction?"
- "What's something that's been tried and didn't work? I'd want to avoid
  repeating it."
- "How does the team feel about [specific thing the role would change]?
  I'd want to make sure I'm not walking into resistance."

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd want to understand how the team feels about the direction — is there
> buy-in, or things that have been tried and didn't work? I'd want to start
> by listening before changing anything."

**What not to raise:** Compensation, org strategy, budget concerns — the
subordinate can't address them and it puts them in an awkward position.

### Peer (fellow IC or same-level manager)

The peer can speak to: collaboration patterns, cross-team friction,
technical reality, how things actually get done. They cannot speak to your
compensation or the hiring decision.

**Best concerns:**
- "I'd want to understand how this team collaborates with [adjacent team] —
  is there friction I should be aware of?"
- "How does work actually get prioritized day-to-day? Is it formal, or
  more ad-hoc?"
- "What's the technical debt situation — is the team able to invest in
  paying it down, or is it all feature work?"

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd want to understand how this team collaborates with adjacent teams —
  is there friction I should be aware of? And how does work get
  prioritized day-to-day?"

**What not to raise:** The peer's personal career dissatisfaction, salary,
or questions that sound like you're evaluating them rather than the role.

### Manager (hiring manager — your prospective boss)

The manager can speak to: scope, resources, team health, expectations,
budget, the actual problem they're hiring you to solve. This is the person
who can address the most substantive concerns.

**Best concerns:**
- "I'd want to understand the resource picture — is the team funded for
  what's being asked, or is this a 'do more with less' situation?"
- "What's the biggest risk to this role succeeding? What would make you
  look back and say 'that didn't work'?"
- "How does this role fit into the team's current structure — am I
  replacing someone, or is this net-new headcount? The dynamics are
  different."
- "What's your management style, and how do you like to be kept in the
  loop? I'd want to make sure I'm a good fit for how you operate."

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd want to understand the resource picture — is the team funded for
> what's being asked? And what's the biggest risk to this role succeeding?
> I'd want to make sure I'm walking in with eyes open."

**What not to raise:** Compensation specifics (recruiter's domain), the
manager's personal performance, or concerns that sound like you're
challenging their judgment rather than understanding their constraints.

### Skip-manager (hiring manager's manager — VP/SVP level)

The skip-manager can speak to: strategic direction, org-level priorities,
funding trajectory, where this team sits in the broader org. They cannot
speak to day-to-day execution or individual team dynamics.

**Best concerns:**
- "I'd want to understand where this team sits in the org's strategic
  priorities — is it a growth area, or is it being maintained?"
- "What's the funding trajectory for this area over the next 12–18 months?
  Is the team growing, stable, or under pressure?"
- "How does this team's work connect to the top-level business goals? I'd
  want to make sure I'm aligned with where the org is actually investing."

**Generic fallback:**
> "I'd want to understand where this team sits in the org's strategic
> priorities — is it a growth area, or being maintained? And what's the
> funding trajectory for this area over the next year?"

**What not to raise:** Tactical concerns about tooling, process, or daily
workflow (that's the manager's or peers' domain), or concerns about the
skip-manager's own position.

### Cross-audience: the concern that works everywhere

If you can only prepare one concern that is safe with any audience:

> "I'd want to understand what success looks like for this role at the
> 12-month mark — what would tell you this was the right hire?"

This works for the same reason the cross-audience question in §11 works:
each person can answer at their own altitude. The recruiter answers at the
process level, the subordinate at the collaboration level, the peer at the
team-contribution level, the manager at the outcomes level, the
skip-manager at the strategic level.

### When to raise a real concern

If you have a genuine concern that could be a dealbreaker — not a
curiosity, but something that would make you decline the offer — raise it
with the **recruiter** privately, not in a panel interview. The recruiter
is the neutral channel for compensation, visa, relocation, and
title-level concerns. Raising a dealbreaker in front of the hiring manager
puts both of you in an awkward position and can short-circuit the process
before you've had a chance to evaluate the full picture.

| "Tell me about yourself." | Present → past → future, anchored in this role | "I'm a [role] who's spent the last [N] years [core work]. Before that I [one line]. I'm here because I want to [direction this role enables]." |
| "Walk me through your resume." | Chronological, ~2 minutes, highlighting transitions and the "why" behind each move; end on why this role is next | "Started in [first role] doing [core work], moved to [next role] because [transition reason], which led to [current role] where I [core achievement]. This role hits all three, which is why I'm here." |
| "What would you do in your first 30/60/90 days?" | Listen → diagnose → ship: 30 days = learn the system and people, 60 days = identify the highest-leverage problem and start, 90 days = deliver something measurable | "First 30 days: listen — meet everyone, learn the system, understand how decisions get made. 60 days: identify the highest-leverage problem and start on it. 90 days: ship something measurable, even if small, to build trust." |

It keeps me [genuine benefit: 'curious' / 'balanced' / 'sharp']." |
| "How do you stay current with technology / industry trends?" | Concrete practices, not vague claims — name specific sources, communities, or habits | "I [specific practice: 'read [specific newsletter/blog]' / 'attend [specific conference]' / 'build small side projects to try new tools']. Recently I [specific recent example]." |
| "What would your previous boss / direct reports say about you?" | Honest, specific, and framed as a strength the interviewer cares about — not a generic "they'd say I'm great" | "My boss would say I [specific strength with evidence: 'own problems end-to-end — I took on the X project and shipped it']. | "Why did you choose this career path?" | A genuine origin story that connects to the present — not a rehearsed narrative; if you're a career changer, name the moment of the pivot and why | "I started in [origin], and what pulled me toward [current field] was [specific moment or realization]. That's still what drives me — [connection to the role's work]." |
| "What's your greatest weakness?" | A real weakness with a concrete mitigation you've already implemented | "I used to [real tendency]. I've addressed it by [specific change], and [evidence it's working]." |
| "What's your greatest strength?" | The strength most relevant to this role, with a concrete example | "[Strength] — at [past role] that meant [specific outcome]." |
| "Why should we hire you?" | Three reasons mapped to the role's stated needs | "Three reasons: [skill this role needs], [evidence you've done it], [fit with the team's gap]." |
| "What are your salary expectations?" | Deflect to their range (see [Salary Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md)) | "I've researched [company] compensation. What does an average offer look like for this level?" |
| "Do you have questions for us?" | Always yes — audience-aware: match the question to who is asking (see [§11](#11-what-questions-do-you-have-for-me--the-audience-aware-question) and [Interview Strategy — Asking Good Questions](interview-strategy.md#asking-good-questions)) | "What does success look like for this role, and what would tell you six months in that this was the right hire?" |
| "What concerns do you have about this role?" | Audience-aware: match the concern to who can address it (see [§12](#12-what-concerns-do-you-have-about-this-role--the-audience-aware-concern)) | "I'd want to understand what success looks like at the 12-month mark — what would tell you this was the right hire?" |
| "Tell me about a time you failed." | A real failure, what you learned, what you changed | "At [role] I [specific failure]. What I learned was [lesson], and I changed [specific behavior] — which is why [later outcome] went better." |
| "Tell me about a conflict at work." | A real disagreement, how you resolved it, the outcome | "I disagreed with [peer] on [issue]. I [specific step to resolve], we landed on [decision], and the outcome was [result]." |
| "What motivates you?" | A specific, honest driver tied to the role's work | "I'm motivated by [specific: 'shipping things people rely on' / 'turning around a struggling system']. This role does that directly." |
| "Are you interviewing elsewhere?" | Honest but bounded (see [Recruiter Communication — Information Boundaries](recruiter-communication.md#information-boundaries-what-not-to-share-with-agency-recruiters)) | "I'm in a few conversations, but I'm being selective — this role is a strong fit and I'd like to see it through." |

### Why only §11 and §12 are audience-specific

The test: does the answer **fundamentally change** based on who is asking,
or does it stay constant with only emphasis shifting? §11 and §12 are
**other-directed** — you are literally asking about or responding to *them*,
so the answer must match what that person can address. Every other question
is **self-directed** — the answer is about you (your story, your weakness,
your strength, your failure, your motivation), and the core doesn't change
by audience. What shifts is at most the altitude — a skip-manager gets the
strategic framing, a peer gets the tactical framing — but that's a dial, not
a different answer.

## The Fallback Principle

The fallback answers above share a common shape:

1. **Forward-looking** — They describe where you're going, not what you're
   escaping.
2. **Role-relevant** — They reference the specific work this role does,
   even when general.
3. **Red-flag-free** — No negativity, no desperation, no threat, no
   hand-waving.
4. **Concrete enough to be credible** — A specific behavior or past
   example, not an abstract claim.

When you cannot personalize, the fallback is the answer. A clean general
answer beats a half-specific one that doesn't quite fit. The worst outcome
is forcing a specific hook that isn't true — the interviewer can tell, and
it signals you'll say whatever it takes to get the job.
