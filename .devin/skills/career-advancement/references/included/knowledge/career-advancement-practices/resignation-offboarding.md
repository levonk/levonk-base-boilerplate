# Resignation and Offboarding

## The Core Insight

How you leave a job matters as much as how you performed in it. The people you
work with will become your **reference network**, your **future colleagues**,
and your **industry reputation**. Resigning gracefully is not about loyalty to
the employer — it is about protecting your own future.

> The industry is smaller than you think. The person you burn on the way out
> may be the person who decides your next offer.

## The Resignation Conversation

### Sequence

Your manager hears it **first**, from you, before anyone else in the company
knows. This is non-negotiable. If your manager learns from a colleague, a
reference call, or a Slack rumor, the relationship is permanently damaged.

| Step | What to do | What not to do |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| 1. Schedule a 1:1 | Ask for a short private meeting; do not hint at the topic in the invite | Don't ambush them in a hallway or a group setting |
| 2. State it clearly | "I'm resigning. My last day will be [date]." | Don't lead with reasons, complaints, or a counter-offer bid |
| 3. Be firm | This is a decision, not a negotiation | Don't hedge ("I'm thinking about leaving…") |
| 4. Don't over-explain | One or two sentences on the reason is enough | Don't deliver a manifesto of grievances |
| 5. Follow up in writing | Send a brief resignation letter the same day | Don't send the letter before the conversation |

### In Person If Possible

For a manager in the same office, do it in person. For a remote manager, do it
on a video call — not email, not Slack. The medium signals respect. A resignation
delivered by email reads as avoidance.

### The Resignation Letter

Keep it short and neutral. The letter is a **legal record**, not a venue for
sentiment or critique.

> Dear [Manager], I am resigning from my position as [title], effective
> [date]. Thank you for the opportunity to work here. I will do everything I
> can to ensure a smooth transition. Sincerely, [Name].

That is the entire letter. No reasons, no feedback, no emotion.

## The Notice Period

| Role level | Standard notice | Notes |
|-----------|-----------------|-------|
| Individual contributor | 2 weeks | The universal default |
| Senior / lead | 2–3 weeks | Allows for deeper handoff |
| Manager / director | 3–4 weeks | Team transition takes longer |
| Executive (VP+) | 4 weeks minimum | Often negotiated as part of departure |

### Don't Shortchange the Notice Period

If you want to preserve the relationship — for references, for a possible
return, for industry reputation — **do not truncate the notice period** to
start your new job two weeks earlier. The cost of a shortened, chaotic handoff
is a manager who remembers you as someone who left them in a lurch.

### When the Employer Walks You Out Immediately

Some companies (especially in finance, sales, and certain tech roles) terminate
access the moment you resign. This is not personal — it is policy. Be prepared:
have personal files backed up **before** you resign, and never take company
data with you (see below).

## The Exit Interview

The exit interview is a **professional courtesy**, not a therapy session. Be
honest but measured.

| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Give specific, actionable feedback | Vent or deliver a grievance dump |
| Name structural issues, not personal vendettas | Attack named individuals |
| Acknowledge what worked well | Pretend everything was fine if it wasn't |
| Keep it professional and brief | Use the exit interview as your last word |

Your feedback may never be acted on. That is not your concern. Your concern is
that **what you say is on the record** — professional, factual, and
unembarrassable if it were ever read back to you.

## Knowledge Transfer

A clean handoff is the single most concrete way to preserve goodwill. The
manager who watches you leave a documented, organized transition will recommend
you again. The manager who inherits a mess will not.

| Artifact | What it contains |
|----------|-----------------|
| Project status doc | Current state, next milestones, risks, owners |
| Runbook / on-call guide | How to operate the systems you owned |
| Code / doc pointers | Where things live, who owns what now |
| Stakeholder map | Who to talk to for each decision area |
| Training sessions | Live walkthroughs with your replacement or team |

Schedule the training sessions **early** in the notice period, not in the last
two days. If your replacement has not been hired, record a walkthrough and
leave the recording with the team.

## What NOT to Do

- **Don't vent on the way out.** Frustration is understandable; broadcasting
  it is unprofessional. Save it for a private conversation with a trusted
  friend outside the company.
- **Don't take company data.** No customer lists, no source code, no internal
  documents. This is the fastest way to destroy your career and invite legal
  action. Personal files only — and even those should be cleanly separated.
- **Don't badmouth the company to colleagues.** It poisons the team's morale,
  reaches management, and becomes your reputation. If colleagues ask why you
  are leaving, keep it high-level: "a great opportunity came up."
- **Don't check out before you leave.** Phoning it in during the notice period
  is visible and remembered. Stay engaged through the last day.
- **Don't recruit your colleagues on the way out.** Even if you are going
  somewhere great, actively poaching from your soon-to-be-former team burns
  the bridge.

## Preserving the Relationship

| Reason to preserve it | Why it matters |
|----------------------|---------------|
| You may want to return | Boomerang employees are common; a good exit makes return possible |
| You need references | Future employers will call this manager and these peers |
| The industry is small | People move between companies; reputations travel |
| Your network compounds | Every colleague is a future connection, referral, or collaborator |

Stay in touch after you leave. A message every few months — a relevant article,
a congratulations on a launch, a quick check-in — keeps the relationship warm
without being transactional.
