# Promotion and Internal Mobility

## The Core Insight

Internal promotion is a **different game** from external job search. Externally,
the market sets your value through competition. Internally, a small group of
decision-makers sets your value through **what they have seen you do** — and
what they have seen is only a fraction of what you have actually done. The
promotion is won or lost in the **visibility gap** between your work and their
perception of it.

> You are not promoted for the work you did. You are promoted for the work
> the decision-makers know you did.

## Sponsorship vs Mentorship

| | Mentor | Sponsor |
|---|--------|---------|
| What they do | Give advice, feedback, perspective | Advocate for you in rooms you are not in |
| Where it happens | 1:1 conversations | Promotion calibrations, talent reviews, staffing decisions |
| Who benefits | You (immediately) | You (via their reputation being staked on you) |
| How you get one | Ask; most people will mentor | Earn; sponsors choose you after you deliver for them |
| Drives promotions? | Indirectly | Yes — this is the primary mechanism |

You need **both**, but sponsorship is what moves promotions. A mentor helps you
grow; a sponsor puts their credibility behind your name when the list is being
made. Without a sponsor, you are relying on the system to notice you — and
systems are not that attentive.

### How to Earn a Sponsor

1. **Deliver exceptional work for them directly.** Sponsors stake their
   reputation on you; they need to have seen your work up close.
2. **Make them look good.** Sponsors advocate for people who reflect well on
   their own judgment.
3. **Ask for bigger scope.** Sponsors want to back people with ambition, not
   people waiting to be noticed.
4. **Be explicit about your goals.** Tell your sponsor what you want next.
   They cannot advocate for a goal they do not know you have.

## Visibility

The **performance review** is not enough. By the time the review cycle arrives,
the promotion decisions have largely been pre-negotiated in calibration
meetings. If decision-makers are hearing about your impact for the first time
in your self-review, you have already lost.

### Year-Round Visibility Practices

| Practice | Frequency | Why it works |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Send a concise "wins" update to your manager | Monthly | Keeps your impact top-of-mind without boasting |
| Present at team / org reviews | Quarterly | Puts your work in front of skip-level leaders |
| Volunteer for cross-team initiatives | Ongoing | Expands the set of leaders who have seen you deliver |
| Document impact in writing, publicly | Ongoing | Creates a searchable record decision-makers can cite |
| Have skip-level 1:1s | Quarterly | Builds direct relationships with the people who approve promotions |

The goal is not self-promotion for its own sake. The goal is to ensure that
when your name comes up in a calibration meeting, **multiple people in the
room have direct knowledge of your work**.

## The Promotion Case

A promotion is a **business case**, not a reward for time served. You are
asking the company to reclassify you at a higher level — which means you must
already be operating at that level.

### Building the Case

| Component | What it contains |
|----------|-----------------|
| Impact documentation | Specific outcomes, with metrics, at the target level |
| Scope evidence | Demonstrating you operated at the next level's scope, not just your current one |
| Business justification | Why promoting you serves the company's goals, not just your career |
| External calibration | Evidence that the market would classify this work at the target level |
| Sponsor endorsement | A senior leader willing to advocate for the case in calibration |

### The "Promo Packet"

Many companies (especially in tech) require a formal **promo packet** — a
document that makes the case to a calibration committee. Treat it like a
resume for the next level:

1. **Lead with impact at the target level.** Not "what I did," but "what I
   did that is already senior-level work."
2. **Quantify everything.** Calibration committees deal in evidence, not
   adjectives (see [Evidence Over Experience](evidence-over-experience.md)).
3. **Include peer and cross-functional input.** A packet with only your
   manager's voice reads as nepotism; a packet with multiple voices reads as
   consensus.
4. **Address the level rubric explicitly.** Map each of your examples to the
   company's published criteria for the target level.

## Internal Transfer

Sometimes the right move is not up but **sideways** — to a different team,
product, or function within the same company.

| When to transfer | When not to |
|-----------------|-------------|
| Your current path is blocked (no headcount, no growth) | You are running from a problem you will recreate elsewhere |
| You want to build a new skill set with lower risk | You have not built credibility in your current role yet |
| A high-growth team is hiring internally | The transfer is lateral in every sense (no new scope) |
| Your current manager is a blocker you cannot remove | You have not tried to fix the relationship first |

### The "Don't Tell Your Manager Too Early" Problem

Internal transfers create a delicate political situation. If you tell your
manager you want to transfer before you have an offer in hand, you may be
perceived as disengaged — and lose opportunities in your current role while
you wait. If you don't tell them and they find out from the hiring manager,
you have burned trust.

| Approach | Risk | Mitigation |
|----------|------|-----------|
| Tell manager early | Perceived as checked-out; lose current opportunities | Only if you have a strong, trusting relationship |
| Tell manager only after interviewing | Surprise; trust damage if discovered | Have the conversation the moment the other team is serious |
| Have the hiring manager inform your manager | Loss of control over the narrative | Insist on telling your manager yourself first |

The safest sequence: explore quietly, and the moment a transfer becomes a
real possibility (not just a curiosity), have an honest conversation with your
manager. Frame it as growth, not escape.

## The "Stale" Problem

Being at the same company too long can **cap your salary and your title**.
Internal raise budgets are typically 3–5% per year; external offers commonly
move compensation 15–30%. Internal promotion cycles are slow and contested;
external offers come with a level set by the market, not by your current
calibration history.

| Signal | What it means |
|--------|---------------|
| You are paid below market for your level | The external market values you higher than your employer does |
| Your title lags your actual scope | You are doing senior work at a junior title |
| Promotions are repeatedly deferred | The internal path is structurally blocked |
| New hires at your level are paid more | The market has moved; your internal raises have not kept up |
| You have not interviewed externally in 3+ years | You do not know your market value |

### The Remedy

You do not have to leave — but you do have to **test the market periodically**.
Even if you stay, an external offer is the strongest possible leverage in an
internal compensation conversation (see [Salary Negotiation](salary-negotiation.md)
and [Counter-Offer Dynamics](counter-offer-dynamics.md) for how to use it
without burning the relationship).
